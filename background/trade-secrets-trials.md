# Trade secrets, racketeering, and split verdicts

Background for feature 3 (the Huawei verdict). The dossier is
stories/03-huawei-verdict.md.

## The two charges are different animals

Conspiracy to steal trade secrets is about conduct: agreeing to
take another company's confidential commercial information and
doing it. Racketeering is about structure: it asks whether the
defendant ran an enterprise through a pattern of criminal acts,
and it carries the heavier penalties and the heavier stigma.
A jury that convicts on the first and acquits on the second is
saying something precise: the conduct happened, the enterprise
theory did not persuade. That precision is why the headline
prints both halves.

## Why the case ran for years

The prosecution of Huawei was never only a courtroom matter: it
sat inside the US-China technology contest, involved an
extradition fight over a senior executive, and was watched by
every company that sells infrastructure to both sides. A
months-long trial in New York was the end of that road, and the
split verdict is a genuinely split ending: a standing
conviction the company must carry, and an acquittal on the
charge that would have defined it as a criminal enterprise.

## The desk's rule for verdicts

Print the split whole or do not feature the story. A headline
that says only "guilty" or only "acquitted" about this verdict
would be a false statement made of true words, and verdicts are
the one genre where readers reliably check. The rule is stated
in PRESS.md (rule 7) because this edition needed it.

## What would change the story

Sentencing, which prices the conviction; an appeal, which could
reopen either half; and any parallel case that cites this
verdict's split as a template.
