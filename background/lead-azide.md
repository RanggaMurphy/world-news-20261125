# Lead azide, and why it is blown up where it sits

Background for grid stories 1 and 3 (the Iowa detonation and
its explainer). Dossiers: stories/04-iowa-detonation.md and
stories/06-iowa-explainer.md.

## What lead azide is

Lead azide is a primary explosive: the kind used to start
detonations, sensitive by design. The record states the
property that drives the whole story: it becomes more
dangerous as it degrades. Old primary explosive is not a thing
you load onto a truck. The standard answer is controlled open
detonation at the site, which is the Army's plan for the 68
barrels, five tons, at the Iowa ammunition plant.

## Why residents were told to stay indoors

Detonating five tons of a lead compound makes a dust plume, and
lead dust is the hazard after the bang is over. The warning to
stay indoors is about the plume, and the plume is where the
record's one open conflict sits: officials gave conflicting
accounts of how far it could travel. The page prints the
conflict rather than a distance, under the house rule that a
safety figure officials disagree on is reported as a
disagreement.

## Why the timing is in the headline

The blast was set for Wednesday morning, the day before
Thanksgiving: a week when the population near the plant is at
home, travelling, or hosting, and when a stay-indoors warning
reaches people in the middle of holiday routines. Timing is
not colour here; it changes who the warning had to reach and
how.

## Why an explainer existed at all

When officials conflict, a report that repeats both accounts
leaves residents with two numbers and no judgement. The wires'
explainer is the genre that exists for that gap: one account of
the plan, the warnings and the reasoning. The edition prints
its headline under the report's, so the page itself performs
the sequence the news performed: first the event, then the
explanation.

## The Thanksgiving variable

A detonation warning is a communication problem before it is an
engineering one, and the holiday changes the communication
problem: residents are travelling, hosting, or away; local
media run holiday staffing; official channels compete with
family logistics for attention. None of that changes the
physics of the plume, and all of it changes whether the warning
arrives. The edition keeps the timing in the headline and in
this briefing because a future reader, meeting the story
without its week attached, would otherwise wonder why a routine
disposal rated two wire pieces and a stay-indoors warning. The
week is the answer.

## Ammunition plants and their neighbours

The Iowa plant is not an abstraction to the people in the
warning's radius: it is the county's standing fact, an employer
and a hazard in the same fence line. Disposal operations like
this one are the moments when that bargain becomes visible to
everyone else. The desk's sympathy in the record runs to
accuracy rather than to any party: residents deserved one plume
account, officials owed them one, and the page prints the gap
between those two sentences as the conflict it was.
