# Story 3 - The Huawei verdict (feature 3)

**Headline as printed:** Huawei found guilty of stealing trade
secrets but acquitted of racketeering

**Position:** Feature 3, right column.

**Carriers:** Al Jazeera, BBC News (two, independently owned:
the cleanest confirmation among the features).

**Region:** United States / China.

**Panel label:** HUAWEI.

## What happened

A New York jury's verdict, after a months-long trial, split the
long-running US prosecution of Huawei in two: the Chinese
technology giant was convicted of conspiracy to steal trade
secrets and acquitted of racketeering. The split is the story:
both sides can present the outcome as a partial win, and the
trade-secrets conviction stands while the heavier charge falls.

## The facts on record

- The trial ran for months in New York.
- Huawei was convicted of conspiracy to steal trade secrets.
- Huawei was acquitted of racketeering.
- Two outlets carried the verdict on the day.

## The carriers, weighed

Two carriers, two owners, two newsrooms: the features' cleanest
confirmation. Verdict copy is also the genre where wire and
newsroom accounts most often diverge in emphasis, one leading
with the conviction, the other with the acquittal. Here both
carriers held both halves in the same order, which is why the
page could print the split as a single headline without
choosing an emphasis of its own.

## Why it is a feature

It is a decision that closes a years-long case, and split
verdicts are the hardest outcomes to headline honestly: the
temptation is to print only the half that suits a narrative.
The carriers' wording holds both halves in one line, and the
page prints that line whole. A verdict with a reversal inside
it is exactly what the third feature slot is for.

## How the headline was chosen

The headline is the split itself, guilty first because the
conviction is the operative outcome (it carries sentence
exposure), acquittal second because it bounds the first. Any
shortening that dropped either half was rejected at the desk:
a half-verdict headline would have been a false statement even
if every word in it were true.

## What was not printed

The counts in detail and the sentencing outlook: not in
two-outlet copy in a form the page could carry, and a headline
page does not do sentencing speculation. The history of the
prosecution (indictments, the extradition fight) is background,
kept in the briefing and the linked reporting.

## Watch next

Sentencing on the trade-secrets conviction, and any appeal:
the split verdict gives both sides something to appeal from.

## The headline, phrase by phrase

"Found guilty of stealing trade secrets" is the conviction,
stated first because it is the half that carries consequences.
"But acquitted of racketeering" is the bound on it, and the
"but" is doing honest work: it tells the reader the two halves
pull in opposite directions before they reach either noun.
Dropping the second half for space was the one shortening the
desk refused outright; a split verdict shortened to one half is
a different verdict.
