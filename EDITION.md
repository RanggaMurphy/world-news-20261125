# The reader's companion - 25 November 2026

What this edition is, how to read it, and the judgements behind
it that the page itself cannot show.

## The shape of the day

The features are three decisions and outcomes from three
systems of power: a military strike that killed a security
chief in Gaza, a South African court convicting a man who tried
to join Russia's war, and a New York jury splitting its verdict
on Huawei. Under them, the grid holds the day's events and its
oddity: a detonation about to happen in Iowa (twice, as report
and explainer), a deadly blast in Hanoi, and a dead pope's diary.

A reader who only reads the features learns what power did. A
reader who only reads the grid learns what happened. The page
is built so that neither reading alone is the edition.

## Why Iowa appears twice

The day produced two distinct pieces about the lead azide
stockpile: AP and the Guardian reported the planned detonation,
and AP and Reuters explained it. The explainer existed because
officials had given conflicting accounts of the dust plume, and
residents needed the questions answered in one place. Printing
both headlines is a true statement about the day's news. The
full reasoning is in the README and in stories/06-iowa-explainer.md;
it is repeated here because it is the first thing readers ask.

## Why two slots are panels

The Hanoi blast and the Iowa explainer arrived as wire copy
without photographs. The paper's rule is that a missing
photograph is marked, not filled: a navy panel with the place
name. The panels are easy to spot (VIETNAM and IOWA), and the
record states plainly that they are drawn. An invented or
borrowed image would have been a small lie told at exactly the
point where the paper asks to be trusted.

## The numbers, and what they are

The strip reads 68, 6, 3. Sixty-eight barrels of lead azide,
the day's most concrete figure, from a story about a controlled
explosion. Six dead in Hanoi, the day's worst toll, from the
wires. Three visions in a diary, the day's strangest number,
printed with its status (the diary says) attached. The strip is
ordered by concreteness, not importance: a count of barrels, a
count of people, a count of claims.

## The date line

The date is set big, centred, directly under the masthead,
because this page circulates as a picture, and a picture that
does not name its day can be mistaken for any day's. The
competition this edition was built for judges pictures of a
specific day; the paper's answer is to carry its day at
near-title scale, where even a thumbnail cannot lose it.

## What the edition cannot do

It cannot update. The Iowa detonation was still ahead when the
page was made; the Hanoi toll was a wire count from a scene;
the Gaza succession was unknown. A one-image daily is a
snapshot with its hour printed on it, and the dossiers'
watch-next notes are the edition's way of admitting, story by
story, exactly where its knowledge stops.

## How to cite this edition

Cite the page as The World Today, 25 November 2026, and the
story by its dossier number. Figures from the strip should be
cited with their labels, which carry their sources: the barrels
to the Army's plan as reported by AP and the Guardian, the
Hanoi toll to the wires, the three visions to the diary. A
figure cited without its label is a figure this edition does
not recognise.

## Corrections and clarifications

None at the time of submission. Under the house rules, any
correction to this edition will be recorded in the next
edition's record, naming this one, and this repository's
history will not be rewritten to hide it. The standing watch
items most likely to force a clarification are the Hanoi
injured count and the Iowa plume distance, both printed here at
the strength the day's record supported and no stronger.

## The competition reader

This edition was built for a reader who sees only the picture:
the make.fun judge, shown two front pages and asked which
better sums up the day. Everything in this companion is
therefore, in a sense, an apology for what a picture cannot
say: why Iowa appears twice, why two slots are panels, why a
diary closes the grid. The picture must stand without the
apology, and the desk's test for every layout choice was
whether it survived being seen by someone who would never read
a word of this file. The documents exist for the second kind of
reader, the one who asks why. A good edition owes both readers
an answer, in the medium each of them actually receives.

## What a second day would change

Nothing in the format and everything in the content. The
features would be re-earned by that day's feeds under the same
rules; the strip would take that day's figures; the panels
would appear only if that day's wires again sent stories
without photographs. What carries over is the discipline: two
outlets or it does not run, splits printed whole, claims
keeping their status words, absences labelled rather than
filled. A reader holding two editions side by side should see
the same paper thinking about two different days.
